What I learned

ANTHRO 2278A-650 · Enhanced Contribution

Personal reflection

Researching this topic helped me separate genetic ancestry from racial and cultural identity more clearly. Before this project, I understood that race was socially constructed, but I had not fully considered how ancestry percentages could still encourage people to think of identity as something measurable through DNA. Graves and Goodman’s distinction between cultural, geographic, and genetic ancestry helped me understand why knowing more about someone’s genetic background does not tell us everything about their identity.

Pat’s experience in Nelson’s (2008) study challenged my assumption that receiving ancestry results would necessarily give someone a clearer sense of belonging. Her results conflicted with family knowledge and created uncertainty. This made me consider how people interpret genetic information alongside stories, relationships, and expectations rather than simply accepting it as a complete answer.

For the website, I chose a comparison table to make the different meanings of ancestry easier to understand. I also used a process diagram to explain how ancestry estimates are produced. One difficulty was simplifying the science without making the results sound more precise than they are. During revision, I changed wording that suggested a test could “identify someone’s ethnicity” to wording explaining that it estimates genetic ancestry through comparisons with reference populations.

This learning connects to the ANTHRO 2278A-650 syllabus outcome to “recognize that race does not exist as a biological category,” as well as the outcome to “Demonstrate the role of culture and society in shaping specific ethnic and racial distinctions.” The process diagram applies the first idea by explaining ancestry estimates as comparisons with reference populations rather than tests of racial membership. The comparison table and Pat’s experience apply the second by showing how family knowledge, cultural relationships, and social expectations shape the meaning people give their results. Applying these outcomes to ancestry testing helped me move beyond defining race as socially constructed and examine how everyday interpretations of scientific information can reinforce racial categories.
