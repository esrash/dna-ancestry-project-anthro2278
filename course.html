Connections to course readings

Three readings from Racism, Not Race connect the science of ancestry testing with the history and social consequences of racial classification.

Cultural, geographic, and genetic ancestry

Chapter 9 distinguishes cultural, geographic, and genetic ancestry. These forms can overlap without being interchangeable (Graves & Goodman, 2022c). A genetic connection cannot describe every tradition, language, relationship, or experience that shapes belonging.

Mathieson and Scally (2020) add a further distinction between genealogical ancestry, inherited DNA, and genetic similarity. Together, these readings help explain why a DNA estimate cannot provide a complete account of identity.

Course reading: DNA and ancestry testing, pp. 179–196 ↗
How racial categories gained biological authority

In “How Did Race Become Biological?,” Graves and Goodman (2022a) trace how racial classifications gained authority through social beliefs, laws, and scientific claims.

This history raises a question for interpreting ancestry tests: why might categories presented as scientific measurements be read as evidence of biological races? My interpretation is that explaining testing methods alone is not enough; the assumption connecting genetic labels to racial boundaries also needs to be examined. Roth et al. (2020) add empirical research on beliefs about racial biology.

Course reading: How did race become biological?, pp. 21–41 ↗
Race, racism, and unequal power

Chapter 8 explains racism through institutions and unequal access to power (Graves & Goodman, 2022b). Recognizing that humans do not form discrete biological races does not erase the consequences of racial classification.

This connection matters for communicating the project's argument: the limits of genetic explanations must be discussed while taking lived experiences of racism seriously. Nelson (2008) and Roth and Ivemark (2018) show how family knowledge, personal goals, and anticipated acceptance shape the interpretation of ancestry results.

Course reading: Institutional and systemic racism, pp. 158–178 ↗
