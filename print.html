INTRODUCTION

DNA ancestry tests
and racial identity

If your ancestry estimate changed, would your identity change too?

This research blog explores what ancestry tests can tell us about our genetic background, the limits of those estimates, and how people interpret their results alongside family history, race, ethnicity, and cultural belonging.

The central argument

DNA results can inform family research. Their meaning emerges through interpretation, family knowledge, and social experience. (Royal et al., 2010; Roth & Ivemark, 2018)

How a sample becomes an estimate

An ancestry result is an inference based on comparisons. It is not a direct reading of race or culture from DNA.

01

Look at genetic variation

Testing examines selected genetic markers in a person's DNA. Different tests follow different patterns of inheritance.

02

Compare reference samples

Patterns are compared with samples from people used to represent particular populations. Who is included in those samples matters.

03

Infer an ancestry estimate

Statistical methods estimate relationships to the reference groups. Labels, samples, and methods shape the result and its uncertainty.

Different tests, different windows

Autosomal DNA

Examines inherited variation across many family lines, rather than one direct maternal or paternal line.

Mitochondrial DNA

Follows a direct maternal line. That line is only one path through a much larger family tree.

Y-chromosome DNA

Follows a direct paternal line in people with a Y chromosome. It does not represent the whole family tree.

Source: Royal et al. (2010).

Three meanings of ancestry

A family connection, inherited DNA, and similarity to a reference sample describe different things. (Mathieson & Scally, 2020)

Genealogical ancestry

Your genealogical ancestors are the people in your family pedigree. A place in that pedigree does not guarantee that a distant ancestor contributed an identifiable segment of DNA to you.

Genetic ancestry

Inherited DNA is a subset of your genealogical history. Recombination and inheritance mean that not every distant genealogical ancestor is represented by an identifiable DNA segment.

Genetic similarity

Similarity describes how your DNA compares with sampled people. The reference samples and comparison method shape the inferred connection; a similarity label is not a complete family tree or a measure of cultural belonging.

Conceptual source: Mathieson & Scally (2020).

Ancestry, race, and identity

These ideas can interact, but they answer different questions. Genetic variation does not establish a set of discrete biological races. (Royal et al., 2010; Mathieson & Scally, 2020)

A genetic label can become a social story.
That does not make the label a biological race.

Racial essentialism treats racial groups as having deep, fixed biological differences. Reading ancestry labels as natural racial boundaries can reproduce that assumption. The question is how people interpret the information. (Roth et al., 2020)

What can the studies tell us?

These are different outcomes. The method matters when deciding what research can tell us.

QUALITATIVE INTERVIEWS

How do people interpret results?

Drawing on 100 interviews across racial backgrounds, Roth and Ivemark describe selective interpretations shaped by desired identities and expectations of others' acceptance. Prior racialization also shapes the options people perceive.

What this supports: an explanation of identity negotiation. Interviews do not provide causal proof or population-wide percentages.

Roth & Ivemark (2018) ↗
RANDOMIZED CONTROLLED TRIAL

Does testing increase racial essentialism?

In a trial among native-born White Americans, testing produced no significant average effect on racial essentialism. Secondary analyses linked different changes to baseline genetic knowledge.

What this supports: evidence about testing in this study population. Knowledge was not randomly assigned; the study does not prove that genetics education prevents racism.

Roth et al. (2020) ↗

What the two experiences reveal

Maria and Pat brought different questions to testing. Maria sought information specific enough to establish the African ethnic connection she wanted to explore; broad migration information did not satisfy that goal. Pat encountered a reported maternal-line match that conflicted with what she already knew about her family. Maria’s response involved seeking a more useful interpretation, while Pat’s involved uncertainty about how to reconcile competing accounts. (Nelson, 2008, pp. 766–769)

Read together, these cases suggest that the meaning of a result depends partly on the question a person brings to testing and the knowledge against which they assess the answer. Greater genetic detail does not automatically produce a clearer sense of belonging. This interpretation also connects with Roth and Ivemark’s account of identity claims shaped by personal aspirations and anticipated social acceptance. (Roth & Ivemark, 2018)

What would you turn to if a DNA result challenged your family story?

Together, the cases suggest that scientific information may leave questions of belonging unresolved because those questions involve specificity, trust, and social relationships. These detailed accounts do not show how all consumers respond.

Connections to course readings

Three readings from Racism, Not Race connect the science of ancestry testing with the history and social consequences of racial classification.

Cultural, geographic, and genetic ancestry

Chapter 9 distinguishes cultural, geographic, and genetic ancestry. These forms can overlap without being interchangeable (Graves & Goodman, 2022c). A genetic connection cannot describe every tradition, language, relationship, or experience that shapes belonging.

Mathieson and Scally (2020) add a further distinction between genealogical ancestry, inherited DNA, and genetic similarity. Together, these readings help explain why a DNA estimate cannot provide a complete account of identity.

Course reading: DNA and ancestry testing, pp. 179–196 ↗
How racial categories gained biological authority

In “How Did Race Become Biological?,” Graves and Goodman (2022a) trace how racial classifications gained authority through social beliefs, laws, and scientific claims.

This history raises a question for interpreting ancestry tests: why might categories presented as scientific measurements be read as evidence of biological races? My interpretation is that explaining testing methods alone is not enough; the assumption connecting genetic labels to racial boundaries also needs to be examined. Roth et al. (2020) add empirical research on beliefs about racial biology.

Course reading: How did race become biological?, pp. 21–41 ↗
Race, racism, and unequal power

Chapter 8 explains racism through institutions and unequal access to power (Graves & Goodman, 2022b). Recognizing that humans do not form discrete biological races does not erase the consequences of racial classification.

This connection matters for communicating the project's argument: the limits of genetic explanations must be discussed while taking lived experiences of racism seriously. Nelson (2008) and Roth and Ivemark (2018) show how family knowledge, personal goals, and anticipated acceptance shape the interpretation of ancestry results.

Course reading: Institutional and systemic racism, pp. 158–178 ↗

Personal reflection

Researching this topic helped me separate genetic ancestry from racial and cultural identity more clearly. Before this project, I understood that race was socially constructed, but I had not fully considered how ancestry percentages could still encourage people to think of identity as something measurable through DNA. Graves and Goodman’s distinction between cultural, geographic, and genetic ancestry helped me understand why knowing more about someone’s genetic background does not tell us everything about their identity.

Pat’s experience in Nelson’s (2008) study challenged my assumption that receiving ancestry results would necessarily give someone a clearer sense of belonging. Her results conflicted with family knowledge and created uncertainty. This made me consider how people interpret genetic information alongside stories, relationships, and expectations rather than simply accepting it as a complete answer.

For the website, I chose a comparison table to make the different meanings of ancestry easier to understand. I also used a process diagram to explain how ancestry estimates are produced. One difficulty was simplifying the science without making the results sound more precise than they are. During revision, I changed wording that suggested a test could “identify someone’s ethnicity” to wording explaining that it estimates genetic ancestry through comparisons with reference populations.

This learning connects to the ANTHRO 2278A-650 syllabus outcome to “recognize that race does not exist as a biological category,” as well as the outcome to “Demonstrate the role of culture and society in shaping specific ethnic and racial distinctions.” The process diagram applies the first idea by explaining ancestry estimates as comparisons with reference populations rather than tests of racial membership. The comparison table and Pat’s experience apply the second by showing how family knowledge, cultural relationships, and social expectations shape the meaning people give their results. Applying these outcomes to ancestry testing helped me move beyond defining race as socially constructed and examine how everyday interpretations of scientific information can reinforce racial categories.

Would a changed estimate change your identity?

A changed ancestry estimate may change what someone knows about their genetic connections, but its meaning for identity depends on family knowledge, cultural relationships, and social experience. (Nelson, 2008; Roth & Ivemark, 2018)

The research shows why ancestry testing can provide useful information without resolving every question about belonging. Genetic ancestry, family pedigree, and cultural identity describe different aspects of a person’s background. (Mathieson & Scally, 2020; Graves & Goodman, 2022c)

Interpreting a result therefore requires asking both how the estimate was produced and what conclusions it can reasonably support. Reference populations and statistical methods shape the inference; the result cannot, by itself, define the whole person. (Royal et al., 2010)
