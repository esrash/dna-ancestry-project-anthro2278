Ancestry, race, and identity

These ideas can interact, but they answer different questions. Genetic variation does not establish a set of discrete biological races. (Royal et al., 2010; Mathieson & Scally, 2020)

Four ways of understanding background and belonging
ConceptWhat it describesWhat a DNA result cannot settle
Genetic ancestryInherited genetic relationships and inferred connections to sampled populations.A complete family history or a final cultural identity.
RaceSocial classifications shaped by history, institutions, and how people are perceived.How someone is racialized in everyday life.
EthnicityIdentification connected to shared histories, traditions, language, or origins.Membership through a genetic percentage alone.
Cultural belongingRelationships, participation, lived experience, and recognition by others.Acceptance within a community.

Synthesis based on Royal et al. (2010), Mathieson & Scally (2020), and Roth & Ivemark (2018).

A genetic label can become a social story.
That does not make the label a biological race.

Racial essentialism treats racial groups as having deep, fixed biological differences. Reading ancestry labels as natural racial boundaries can reproduce that assumption. The question is how people interpret the information. (Roth et al., 2020)

What can the studies tell us?

These are different outcomes. The method matters when deciding what research can tell us.

QUALITATIVE INTERVIEWS

How do people interpret results?

Drawing on 100 interviews across racial backgrounds, Roth and Ivemark describe selective interpretations shaped by desired identities and expectations of others' acceptance. Prior racialization also shapes the options people perceive.

What this supports: an explanation of identity negotiation. Interviews do not provide causal proof or population-wide percentages.

Roth & Ivemark (2018) ↗
RANDOMIZED CONTROLLED TRIAL

Does testing increase racial essentialism?

In a trial among native-born White Americans, testing produced no significant average effect on racial essentialism. Secondary analyses linked different changes to baseline genetic knowledge.

What this supports: evidence about testing in this study population. Knowledge was not randomly assigned; the study does not prove that genetics education prevents racism.

Roth et al. (2020) ↗
